Human First · Course guide

Human First: AI Literacy for High School

A short course that teaches 9th graders how to use AI well, how to use it honestly, and how it works. This guide explains the course for teachers, school leaders, and families.

Grade 9 9 modules 15 min each About 2¼ hours total Self-paced, done alone No coding needed
01

Overview

Human First is nine short web pages, one per module. Each page takes about 15 minutes. A student reads short sections, tries one hands-on activity, and takes a quick quiz, all on their own and at their own pace.

The course is built so a teacher with no computer science background can run it. Every hard word is defined right where it first appears. There are no group activities, games, or debates. Students in grade 9 take it first, and short follow-up lessons in grades 10–12 can build on it later.

  • Who it's for: 9th-grade students in Wisconsin public schools. No prior experience needed.
  • Where it lives: on a free website (GitHub Pages), linked from Canvas, the school's online classroom system.
  • What students need: a school Chromebook or any device with a web browser.
  • What makes it original: the H1–H5 scale as the honesty system, Wisconsin's human-centered AI guidance, and every page, activity, and quiz written for this course.
02

Three principles

Everything in the course serves one of these three goals. Effectiveness matters most.

Most important

Effectiveness

Using AI well: writing clear prompts (the instructions you type to an AI), judging the answers, and using AI as a study partner instead of a ghostwriter (something that secretly does your work).

The rules

Integrity

Knowing when AI is allowed and proving your work is your own. Every assignment gets an H1–H5 label that says how much of the work must be human.

The foundation

Literacy

Understanding how AI works and where it fails, such as making things up, treating some people unfairly, and collecting your personal information.

03

The H1–H5 scale

Every assignment carries one label from H1 to H5. The label tells students how much of the work must be their own. H stands for Human. A bigger number means more human work.

The scale comes from a Stanford University research project that studied how workers and AI share tasks on the job. This course uses it for schoolwork.

LevelWhat it meansSchool exampleOn graded work?
H5All you. No AI at all.A test, in-class writing, a personal reflectionYes. The default.
H4You lead, AI helps a little. Small help, like a definition or a quiz on your notes.Your essay, after AI quizzed you on the bookYes, with a disclosure
H3You and AI share. Both add real ideas.A project where AI suggests ideas and you choose, change, and add to themOnly if the teacher labels it H3, with a disclosure and chat log
H2AI does it, you check it."Find the mistakes in this AI answer"Only in AI lessons, where the AI's answer is what you're studying
H1All AI. Nobody checks.Copying an AI answer and turning it inNever

The big rule: H1 is never allowed on schoolwork. Handing something in means "this is mine, and I stand behind it," so a person must always own the work.

No label means H5. If an assignment doesn't say, AI is not allowed.

Three quick questions to pick a level

  1. Is the point to show what I can do? (my own skill, voice, or thinking) → H5.
  2. If I deleted everything the AI gave me, would my work still be complete? Yes, just a little rougher → H4. No, ideas would be missing → H3.
  3. Did the AI write it while I only fixed small mistakes? → H2. Not allowed on graded work.

Label for the top of every assignment

AI level: H4. You lead, AI helps a little. You may use ChatGPT to quiz yourself or check definitions. The writing must be your own. Fill in the AI disclosure at the end.

Student AI disclosure

A disclosure is a short note telling the teacher exactly how AI was used. Students paste it at the end of any H4 or H3 work.

I used [tool] on [date] to [what I asked it to do]. I kept / changed / didn't use [what], because [why]. I can explain everything I'm handing in. Level: H__

How the scale connects to the school's honesty rules

Board policyWhat it means with AI
1. Submitting another person's work as one's ownHanding in more AI work than the H-level allows counts as someone else's work. Always fill in the disclosure.
2. Providing or accepting specific test contentTests are always H5. Never type test questions into AI.
3. Using technology in a manner not specified by the teacherThe H-label is the teacher saying how. No label means H5.
4. Using concealed notes on testsHaving AI open during an H5 test counts the same as hidden notes.

05

How a module page works

Every module page follows the same pattern, so students know what to expect. Module 1 is the model.

Read · 2–4 min

Short sections

Plain language with key words defined.

Try · 3–4 min

One activity

An interactive tool built into the page.

Check · 3 min

Quick quiz

5–6 questions with instant feedback.

Optional

Explore more

Free extras tied to a step on that page.

  • Time: 15 minutes or less. Each step shows its time.
  • Words: any word a 9th grader might not know is defined where it first appears, in parentheses or in a "Key words" list.
  • AI level: each page shows its H-level at the top.
  • Explore more: each extra is labeled with the step it goes deeper on and needs no account.
  • Privacy: pages save nothing and collect nothing. Activities run inside the student's own browser.

07

For families

Your student will take Human First, a short course of nine 15-minute lessons about artificial intelligence (AI). They'll learn how to get genuinely useful help from AI, how to use it honestly, and how it works. No coding is needed.

  • Honesty comes first. Every assignment has an H1–H5 label saying how much must be your student's own work. Tests are always H5: no AI at all.
  • Tools. Some lessons use ChatGPT. OpenAI requires users under 18 to have a parent's permission, so we'll ask for yours. Your student will not use Claude or other tools that require users to be 18.
  • Privacy. Students learn never to type personal details, like their full name, address, or photos of other people, into AI.
  • Saying no is fine. Every activity has a no-AI version, and your student loses no points.

09

Open questions for administration

  • Claude approval: Claude requires users to be 18. Should the approved-tool list say "staff only" for Claude?
  • ChatGPT data agreement: students would use personal accounts. Does the district want a data-privacy agreement or a school version instead?
  • Permission slips: who collects and stores them? Does an existing technology form already cover this?
  • School-wide H-labels: should every class use the H1–H5 labels so students see the same rules everywhere?
  • Board policy: should the honesty policy name AI-written work and the disclosure directly?
  • AI checkers: will the school agree not to discipline students based on AI-checker scores alone?
  • Placement: where do the nine lessons live: advisory, an existing class, or Academic and Career Planning time?
  • Standards codes: who will add the verified Wisconsin code numbers?
10

Word list

AI
Artificial intelligence: a computer program that does things we usually expect a person to do, like write or recognize faces.
Chatbot
A program you type messages to that types back.
LLM
Large language model: an AI that learned patterns from huge amounts of writing and predicts the next word.
AI agent
An AI that takes actions toward a goal using tools like web search or online forms.
Prompt
The instructions you type to an AI.
Disclosure
A short note telling the teacher exactly how you used AI.
H1–H5 scale
A five-step label showing how much of a task is human work. H5 = all you.
Integrity
Being honest about your work.
Bias
An unfair leaning for or against a group of people.
Digital footprint
The trail of information you leave online.
